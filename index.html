Approaches

Optical computing (for AI)
Light doing part of a neural network's arithmetic, usually matrix–vector products, with electronics converting data in and out, applying nonlinearities and storing results. Distinct from optical interconnect, which only moves bits.Explained in: OptAI 01 · what this series means by optical computing · OptAI 01 · what is not optical computing here
Interference, propagation and square-law detection
The three facts optical computing rests on: coherent fields add as complex amplitudes (a weighted sum), propagation through optics applies a fixed linear transform, and detectors measure power, losing sign and phase unless a reference is mixed in.Explained in: OptAI 01 · three physical facts
Diffractive networks
Stacks of engineered diffractive layers whose pixels are the weights; light passing through computes a fixed linear network. Strongest on vision, where the input is already light: ACCEL and Taichi report peta-operation and 160 TOPS/W figures by their own definitions.Explained in: OptAI 01 · diffractive networks · OptAI 03 · when the data is already light
MZI-mesh neural network
A programmable grid of Mach–Zehnder interferometers applying a matrix to coherent light: N(N−1)/2 MZIs per unitary, each path crossing about N of them, so errors and loss compound with size. Shen et al. ran a vowel classifier on 56 MZIs at 76.7% against 91.7% simulated.Explained in: OptAI 01 · coherent MZI meshes · OptAI 01 · interactive mesh errors · OptAI 02 · scaling the array
Incoherent WDM crossbar
One input per wavelength, weighted by attenuators, rings or phase-change cells and summed on a photodiode: robust to phase but needing many wavelengths and two detectors for signed values. The photonic tensor core of Feldmann et al. is the best-known example.Explained in: OptAI 01 · incoherent and wavelength-multiplexed engines
Photoelectric multiplication
Encoding both operands in light and multiplying them by interference on a balanced detector, with time integration summing the products. It allows very large networks and a shot-noise floor that can be as low as 50 zJ per MAC in theory.Explained in: OptAI 01 · photoelectric multiplication · OptAI 02 · the photon floor
The nonlinearity problem
Optics is linear at these intensities, so activations are usually applied electronically between optical layers, at a conversion cost per layer. On-chip optoelectronic nonlinearities have run small deep networks in 410–570 ps.Explained in: OptAI 01 · the nonlinearity problem · OptAI 03 · latency niches

Precision and energy

Reuse: MACs per conversion
An N × N matrix–vector product is N2 MACs for 2N conversions, so each conversion is shared by N/2 MACs and converter energy per MAC falls as 1/N. Weights that change for every vector lose this reuse.Explained in: OptAI 02 · the operation and its reuse · OptAI 03 · the conditions for a win
Weight storage in optics
How an optical array holds its weights: thermo-optic phase shifters (continuous heater power), phase-change cells (non-volatile, few levels, slow writes), electro-optic shifters, or modulator pixels. Static power per weight is paid at every use.Explained in: OptAI 02 · storing the weights · OptAI 02 · static power and utilisation
The photon floor
Shot noise falls as 1/√n with n detected photons, so matching the quantisation noise of an E-bit converter needs about 3·4E photons per detection: about 200,000 (25 fJ at 1550 nm) at 8 bits. Each extra bit quadruples it.Explained in: OptAI 02 · the photon floor · OptAI 02 · interactive energy per MAC
Conversion floor
The energy of the DACs and ADCs around an optical engine, each about Walden FoM × 2ENOB per sample and shared by N MACs. It, not the optics, usually dominates, and it sets the minimum energy any claim must cover.Explained in: OptAI 02 · why converters dominate · OptAI 04 · interactive claim checker
Static power per weight
Power drawn to hold a weight whether or not it computes, costing P/f per use at vector rate f: 1 mW at 1 GHz is 1 pJ per MAC, more than an 8-bit digital MAC at 45 nm.Explained in: OptAI 02 · static power and utilisation · OptAI 03 · interactive win map
Digital MAC energy
The yardstick: about 0.2 pJ for an 8-bit multiply and 0.03 pJ for an add at 45 nm, against 10 pJ for a 64-bit SRAM access and 1.3–2.6 nJ for a DRAM access (Horowitz). An H100 at peak dense FP8 is about 0.7 pJ per MAC at board level.Explained in: OptAI 02 · the digital yardstick · OptAI 03 · memory and Amdahl
Calibration and drift
Measuring and correcting each device's fabrication errors after manufacture, and holding the result against temperature. Self-configuring meshes tolerate split ratios from 85:15 to 15:85; heat moves every phase.Explained in: OptAI 02 · calibration and drift · OptAI 01 · coherent MZI meshes

Where it wins

Conditions for an optical win
Large matrices, low precision, static reused weights, low static power, high utilisation and weights held near the optics. Analyses place the photonic advantage at N > 500 and noise precision of 4 bits or fewer.Explained in: OptAI 03 · the conditions for a win · OptAI 03 · interactive win map
Memory wall for optical compute
A DRAM access costs thousands of digital MACs, so an optical engine that makes MACs cheap but still fetches its weights saves little. Optical engines must be weight-stationary, and large models must be tiled and reloaded.Explained in: OptAI 03 · memory and Amdahl · OptAI 02 · the digital yardstick
1/d scaling of optical energy
In an optical Transformer, optical energy per MAC falls as 1/d with model width d, because conversions and photons are shared by more MACs. Anderson et al. project about 100× for the largest current Transformers and over 8,000× at a quadrillion parameters (modelled, not measured).Explained in: OptAI 03 · the scaling argument
Latency niches
Problems where the time for light to cross a chip matters: iterative solvers looping through one matrix (Ising problems at up to 1 GHz and 3 ns per cycle), control and signal processing, and sub-nanosecond classification.Explained in: OptAI 03 · latency niches · OptAI 04 · Lightelligence
Optical AI scorecard
This series' judgement by workload: strongest for vision on light that arrives optically, plausible for CNN inference and iterative solvers, conditional on memory for LLM projections, poor for attention and hardest for training.Explained in: OptAI 03 · a scorecard

Reading the landscape

Company claim
A performance figure stated by a company in a press release, product page or funding announcement. This series quotes, links and labels each one, and asks what operation, precision, power, baseline and workload it rests on.Explained in: OptAI 04 · how to read a company's claim · OptAI 04 · questions to ask
Claim checker
Turning a claimed throughput and power into energy per MAC and comparing it with the conversion floor for an assumed array width and precision: a test of consistency, not truth, that shows what a claim must assume.Explained in: OptAI 04 · interactive claim checker
Adaptive block floating point (ABFP)
A number format in which a block of values shares an exponent; Lightmatter's 2025 processor reports 16-bit ABFP operations computed with analogue photonic cores, reaching near-electronic precision on ResNet and BERT.Explained in: OptAI 04 · Lightmatter
The pull towards interconnect
Companies that began in optical compute increasingly also sell optical interconnect, which moves bits digitally with no analogue precision to defend; Marvell's acquisition of Celestial AI's Photonic Fabric is the largest deal listed.Explained in: OptAI 04 · the interconnect companies · OptAI 04 · the pull towards interconnect
Levels of evidence
From strongest to weakest: a peer-reviewed system running real models; peer-reviewed results on selected tasks; products announced with claims but no published method; funding with targets.Explained in: OptAI 04 · what has been shown
